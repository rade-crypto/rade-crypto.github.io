A text-based study · Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek and the ancient historians

Dating Daniel's Prophecies

When was Daniel written? This study measures the language of the text against dated corpora: Persian-era Aramaic papyri from Elephantine, Ezra, the whole Hebrew Bible, the Dead Sea Scrolls, and Greek literature, papyri and inscriptions. A separate second part checks the text's information against the ancient historians.

What the language says

Measured against dated texts, Daniel's language is not 6th-century language. Its Aramaic is later than the Aramaic of the Persian-era papyri and of Ezra's documents, though older-looking than the Aramaic of Qumran. Within the book, the vision of chapter 7 is measurably later than the court stories of chapters 2–6. Its Hebrew groups with Ezra, Nehemiah, Chronicles and Esther, not with Ezekiel, Haggai and Zechariah, who really wrote in the 6th century. Its vocabulary is full of Persian administrative terms, even in stories set at Nebuchadnezzar's court. One of its Greek instrument names has its instrument sense first attested in the 2nd century BCE. The language fits stories written in the late Persian or early Hellenistic period and visions written later still. The separate information check in Part two puts the visions at 167–164 BCE.

How it was done

Primary data only

Every result here comes from data that can be checked. The language corpora are:

  • the full Masoretic text of the Hebrew Bible with word-level grammar (ETCBC database);
  • the morphologically tagged Dead Sea Scrolls, about 500,000 words of Hebrew and Aramaic from the 3rd to 1st centuries BCE;
  • {{imp_corpus}}
  • 36 million words of Greek literature, 5 million words of Greek papyri and 3.9 million words of Greek inscriptions.

All language counts use the consonantal text only; the medieval vowel signs are never used as evidence. No interpretive commentary was used to decide anything.

Several of these tests were not practical before. One is a dating model that reads every word of 34 dated Hebrew texts and is checked by predicting each text while blind to it. Another is a control that scores the scroll copies of old books against their medieval counterparts. A third is a direct feature-by-feature comparison with the Persian-era Aramaic papyri, and a fourth is a full-corpus search for when a Greek word changed meaning. All code and outputs are in the project folder listed at the end.

1 · Aramaic

Later than the Persian-era papyri and Ezra, earlier-looking than Qumran

Several grammatical features changed between the Aramaic of the Persian empire and the Aramaic of the Hellenistic period. Each was counted in the consonantal text alone, with no medieval vowels, in four bodies of text:

  • {{imp_short}}
  • Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26, documents the text itself dates to the late 6th and 5th centuries BCE;
  • Daniel 2:4b–7:28;
  • about 46,000 words of non-biblical Aramaic from Qumran, written in the 3rd to 1st centuries BCE;
  • for the late end of the timeline, about 177,000 words of the Targums Onkelos and Jonathan (2nd–3rd centuries CE).

Grammar. On the four grammatical features that changed ("their," "your" plural, "they," "these"), the 5th-century papyri use the old form in all 268 cases. Ezra uses the later form in 17 of 47 cases, Daniel in 53 of 66, Qumran Aramaic in 832 of 848, and the Targums of the 2nd–3rd centuries CE in all 4,727. Daniel writes "their" only as -hwn (36 of 36); in the papyri it is -hm or -hwm 97 times out of 97.

The causative prefix goes the other way. Counting only the verb forms in which the prefix is always written, Daniel has the old h- in 93 of 96 cases, the same proportion as the papyri (185 of 193) and Ezra (20 of 21). The literary Aramaic of Qumran has switched to ʾ- (235 of 298), and the Targums almost completely (714 of 716). On this feature Daniel looks Persian-era. Formal inscriptions from Mt Gerizim (3rd–2nd century BCE) also keep h- (24 of 24), so the old prefix survived in formal writing into the Hellenistic period.

Spelling. Daniel always uses the later spellings with d for older z (dy "which," dnh "this") and ʿ for older q (ʾrʿ "earth"), as do Ezra and Qumran. The papyri keep the old spellings about 98% of the time. Copyists can update spelling, so this counts for less than grammar.

Not a time marker. The reflexive prefix (ht- vs ʾt-) cannot date anything. The papyri already write ʾt- (33 of 34), while Daniel and Ezra prefer ht-, a convention of Biblical Aramaic. An earlier version of this report used it, together with a looser count of the causative prefix, to argue that chapter 7 is later than chapters 2–6. With valid features the difference disappears (2 of 11 later forms vs 9 of 106, p = 0.28), so that claim is withdrawn.

Was the text modernised by later scribes? Qumran copies of Daniel from the late 2nd century BCE onward have the same later forms as the Masoretic text, in about the same proportions. There is one exception: 4QDana writes "from them" with the older -hm twice (2:41–42), where the Masoretic text has -hwn. Some standardisation happened in copying, but the later forms were already dominant in the earliest surviving copies.

Taken together, Daniel's Aramaic grammar is later than the Persian-era papyri and later than Ezra's documents. Its causative prefix is still old-fashioned, unlike Qumran's literary Aramaic. That combination fits composition in the late Persian or early Hellenistic period, or a later writer using a conservative literary standard. It does not fit a 6th-century author, whose Aramaic should be at least as old as the 5th-century papyri on every feature.

2 · Hebrew

It reads like Ezra, Nehemiah and Chronicles

A dating model was trained on 34 texts with broadly agreed dates. They run from Samuel–Kings through the 6th-century prophets and the Persian-era books to 17 compositions from Qumran. The model uses 89 features chosen to be insensitive to genre: frequencies of closed-class words (prepositions, conjunctions, particles, pronouns), verb-stem proportions and suffix rates. Spelling was left out entirely. Each text was dated by a model that had never seen it. The typical error was {{mae}} years, and 74% of texts were placed in the right period.

As a control, the Dead Sea Scroll copies of Samuel, Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel were scored. They sit next to their medieval Masoretic counterparts, so the model is measuring the language, not the database it came from.

Daniel's Hebrew as a whole comes out at about 300 BCE. Its nearest neighbours are Nehemiah, Ezra, Chronicles and the Qumran Hodayot. The period classifier gives it a 4% probability of belonging with the 6th-century texts. Chapters 8 and 10, which are vision reports in the style of Ezekiel and Zechariah, lean earlier. Chapters 11–12 lean later. The single-text error is wide, so language alone cannot tell the 4th century from the 2nd. It does set Daniel apart from its supposed contemporaries.

Two more checks of the model. The Hebrew inscriptions from Lachish, Arad, Siloam, Mesad Hashavyahu and Ketef Hinnom, dated by archaeology to about 700–587 BCE, were never used in training. Scored blind, they came out at about 600 BCE, closest to Samuel, Hosea and Haggai. The Community Rule, War Scroll and Hodayot are each annotated in two different databases, and each copy was matched to its own counterpart as nearest neighbour. Adding Mishnaic Hebrew (Pirqe Avot, Mekhilta, c. 200–250 CE) as a later anchor leaves Daniel where it was, at about 340 BCE.

Word choices that changed over time

The clearest evidence is in places where a writer had to choose between two equivalent forms. Because the measure is a choice, genre matters little.

For "kingdom," Daniel always uses מלכות (16 of 16), as Esther and Mishnaic Hebrew do. The 6th-century prophets almost always use ממלכה. Daniel writes the long form of "and I did…" (ואתפללה) 56% of the time, against 3% in the 6th-century texts, 50% in Ezra–Nehemiah and 57% at Qumran. Daniel never uses the Mishnaic relative ש (0 of 47; Mishnaic Hebrew 99%), so its Hebrew is late literary Hebrew, not the Hebrew of the rabbis. An earlier version also counted object pronouns attached to the verb as a late trait. The pre-exilic Hebrew letters prefer them too (14 of 17), so that difference reflects register, not date, and it is no longer used.

3 · Loanwords, Greek and names

Persian titles, Greek instruments, later spellings

Persian loanwords

Daniel uses 19 Persian words (69 occurrences), most of them Achaemenid administrative terms: satrap, treasurer, judge, magistrate, counsellor, minister, law (dāta), mystery (rāza). In 3:2–3 a whole list of Persian titles staffs Nebuchadnezzar's court (605–562 BCE), decades before the Persian empire existed. In Hebrew, Daniel shares פרתמים "nobles" only with Esther, and אפדן "palace" (11:45) is the Old Persian apadāna.

Greek instruments (Dan 3:5–15)

qytrs = kitharis (Homer onward, so no help). psntryn = psaltērion, first attested c. 300 BCE (Theophrastus, Hist. pl. 5.7.6). swmpnyh = symphōnia. In 85 occurrences from before the Hellenistic age it always means harmony or agreement, never an instrument. Its first concrete musical use is in Polybius, describing Antiochus IV dancing to it (26.1, 30.26). As a named instrument in a list it appears only in the Greek translations of Daniel. The 88,202 Greek inscriptions searched agree. There συμφωνία means "concord" in treaties from 257 BCE, and a musical sense ("a band") appears only in the 2nd century CE. psaltērion appears once, in a Delos temple inventory of 146/5 BCE. Much Hellenistic writing is lost, so this is strong but not conclusive.

4b · The Greek translations

Fixed visions, fluid stories

Daniel survives in two Greek versions: the Old Greek, translated around 100 BCE, and Theodotion, a later revision that follows the Hebrew and Aramaic closely. Comparing the two verse by verse (Rahlfs edition, lemma overlap, with the Greek additions in chapter 3 left out) shows where the Old Greek translator's source text differed from ours.

Around 100 BCE, then, the court stories of chapters 4–6 still circulated in alternative forms, while the visions had a fixed text. That fits stories with a longer, older life of their own, and visions composed recently as a single work. The Old Greek also translates "ships of Kittim" in 11:30 as "the Romans (Ῥωμαῖοι) will come." Its translator, two generations after the events, read the verse as the Roman intervention of 168 BCE.

4 · Text reuse

What Daniel draws on

A search for content-word sequences and rare words that Daniel shares with other books turned up these borrowings:

  • Daniel 1:1–2 shares wording with 2 Kings 24:1 ("Jehoiakim became his servant three years") and 2 Chronicles 36:6–7 (the Temple vessels placed in the king's temple in Babylon). Chronicles is a 4th-century book.
  • "The first year of Cyrus king of Persia" (1:21; 10:1) repeats 2 Chronicles 36:22 and Ezra 1:1.
  • Daniel 9:2 reads Jeremiah's seventy years "in the books," treating Jeremiah as written scripture. 2 Chronicles 36:21 joins the same two ideas.
  • "Ships of Kittim" (11:30) and "the latter days" (10:14) come from Balaam's oracle (Numbers 24:14, 24).
  • דראון "contempt" (12:2) occurs only here and in Isaiah 66:24, a post-exilic chapter. The "wise" who "make many righteous" (12:3) echo Isaiah 52:13 and 53:11.
  • The man "clothed in linen" (10:5; 12:6–7) and his burnished-bronze appearance (10:6) come from Ezekiel 1:7 and 9–10.

By the late 2nd or early 1st century BCE the book was scripture at Qumran: 4Q174 quotes Daniel 12:10 "as it is written in the book of Daniel the prophet."

Part two

Checking the information

Everything above is about the language alone. The three sections below are a separate kind of test: they compare what the text says with the historical record. This depends on deciding which historical events a passage refers to. For Daniel 8 and 11 the text names its own subjects (Media-Persia, Greece), so the correlation is shared by conservative and critical readers, but it is still a reading of the text rather than a measurement of its language.

5 · Information check: the predictions

Sharp up to 166 BCE, wrong after it

Daniel 8 and 11 are the kingdom visions that name their own subjects: the ram is "the kings of Media and Persia," the goat is "the king of Greece" (8:20–21), and chapter 11 begins with the kings of Persia and the coming of Greece (11:2). That lets each verse be lined up with the history it describes. The text was divided into 23 passages and 107 separate claims. Each claim was checked against the ancient sources and scored hit partial miss, or set aside as vague when too general to test.

Two patterns

The focus sharpens toward 167 BCE. The whole Persian empire, 220 years and ten kings, gets {{pers_words}} words: three unnamed kings and a rich fourth who attacks Greece. Then the text skips 142 years without a word. The ten years of Antiochus IV up to 166 BCE get {{a4_words}} words, about a hundred times as many per year. They take in his first and second Egyptian campaigns, a Roman envoy turning him back ("ships of Kittim," 11:30, the Day of Eleusis in Polybius 29.27), the plunder of the Temple, and the "abomination of desolation" set on the altar in December 167 (1 Maccabees 1:54).

Then the accuracy collapses. Daniel 11:40–45 continues with the same king. It predicts a final war with Egypt, the conquest of Egypt, Libya and Cush, and his death in the land of Israel "between the sea and the glorious holy mountain." None of it happened. Rome had already forbidden another Egyptian war. Antiochus went east, and every ancient source puts his death in Persia or Babylonia (Polybius 31.9: "he died at Tabae in Persia"). A Babylonian king list records the news of his death in the month Kislev of 164 BCE. The Temple's rededication that same month is not mentioned as having happened; 8:14 and 12:11–12 still look forward to it.

A Bayesian change-point test on the claims, ordered by date, puts the break at about 165 BCE, with 95% of the probability between 166 and 165. If partial matches are counted as failures, the break moves to about 167 BCE. Either way the break falls in a single three-year window.

The futurist reading. Some readers take 11:36–45 or 11:40–45 as a jump to a far-future figure. The Hebrew gives no signal of a new subject: "the king" and "the king of the north" continue from the preceding verses. Even on that reading the result stands: the text is precise up to the 160s BCE and then stops being checkable. A 6th-century prophet would have no reason to see the 170s in close-up and the Persian empire as a blur.

6 · Information check: the 6th-century setting

Hazy about the 6th century, exact about the 2nd

The stories and dates that frame the visions describe the 6th century, the author's supposed present. Checked against Babylonian and Persian records, they show a mix of authentic memory and error. That is the reverse of the pattern in chapter 11, where the 2nd century is right in detail.

7 · Information check: the day counts

Three different numbers, all too long

1 Maccabees dates the altar's desecration to December 167 BCE and the rededication to December 164 BCE, exactly three years later (1,094–1,104 days). Daniel gives three different figures. Counted from the Maccabean dates:

Daniel's figureCountedLands aboutWhat happened
2,300 evenings and mornings = 1,150 days (8:14)back from the rededicationOct 167 BCEplausible start of the suppression of the daily offering

1,335 days (12:12)from the desecrationAug 163 BCEnothing

A writer who knew the date of the rededication would be expected to give the real interval. Successive, lengthening figures look like a hope recalculated while the outcome was still unknown. This is an inference; the arithmetic itself is in the project files.

8 · The other side

Evidence that points earlier, and how much it weighs

  • Belshazzar. The author knew that Babylon's last ruler on the spot was Belshazzar, which the Greek historians had forgotten. This shows access to genuine Babylonian tradition. Such traditions were still alive in the Hellenistic period: Berossus wrote c. 280 BCE, and the Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus preserves another one.
  • Conservative Aramaic. On every measured feature Daniel's Aramaic is older-looking than the Qumran literature. It keeps the old causative prefix and uses object-first order more than Ezra. This argues against a date as late as the 1st century and fits stories older than the visions.
  • Early acceptance. Copies of Daniel exist from the late 2nd century BCE, and Qumran calls it "Daniel the prophet." Fifty years is short, but enough for a book whose central prediction, the restoration of the Temple, came true within months.
  • Daniel 8:25. "He shall be broken, but not by human hand" fits Antiochus' death by illness. It names no place, and it sits beside 11:45, which names the wrong one.

These points explain the older material in the court stories. They do not explain why precision peaks in the 160s, why the predictions fail after 165 BCE, or why the Hebrew of the visions is later than that of the real 6th-century writers.

9 · Conclusion

Verdict and confidence

From the language alone

Not 6th-century. The Aramaic grammar is later than the 5th-century papyri and Ezra on four features, though its causative prefix is still old-fashioned. The Hebrew groups with Ezra, Nehemiah, Chronicles and Esther, and the same model dates the pre-exilic inscriptions correctly. Persian administrative vocabulary runs through the book, and a Greek instrument name is attested in that sense only from the 2nd century BCE. Language cannot fix a decade: the Hebrew model's error is about ±120 years. Confidence that the book is later than the 6th century: high.

Adding the information check

The predictions of chapters 8 and 11 are exact up to 166 BCE and wrong after 165 BCE, and the day counts outrun the real events. This narrows the visions to 167–164 BCE, before the Temple's rededication and Antiochus IV's death in late 164. The court stories keep some older Babylonian memories (Belshazzar, Nabonidus at Teima). Confidence in 167–164 BCE for chs. 8–12: high; in a 4th–3rd century date for chs. 2–6: moderate.

What would change this

A manuscript of Daniel 11 dated before about 200 BCE. A Babylonian tablet naming a Median king Darius between Nabonidus and Cyrus. A Greek or Aramaic text from before 330 BCE using symphōnia for an instrument. Or an ancient report of Antiochus IV dying in Judea.

Limits

The Hebrew model's error for a single text is large (about ±120 years), and genre still leaks into it. Ezra's Aramaic is documentary and Qumran's is literary. Scoring claims involves judgment, so every claim and its evidence is listed above for audit. The Greek search covers literature, papyri and a cleaned 88,000-inscription subset of PHI, but not the subscription-only TLG. {{imp_limit}}
